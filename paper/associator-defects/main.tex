\title[Square-root-free associator defects]{Square-root-free associator defects in octonion algebras over finite fields, with a mutation-tested pentagon oracle}

\author{[Author names withheld for review]}

\date{October 1, 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
For an octonion algebra $\C$ over a field of characteristic different from~$2$
and elements $x,y$ of nonzero norm, we study the defect operator
$\Delta(x,y)=L_xL_yL_{xy}^{-1}$, a square-root-free replacement for the
normalized operator $U_xU_yU_{xy}^{-1}$, $U_x=L_x/\sqrt{N(x)}$, which is
unavailable over finite fields. We prove that $\Delta(x,y)$ is a
special orthogonal transformation of $(\C,N)$ that fixes pointwise the unital
subalgebra generated by $x$ and $y$. When that subalgebra is a nondegenerate
quaternion subalgebra $Q$, the defect is therefore an element of
$\SO(Q^\perp)$, a four-dimensional rotation group, rather than merely of the
stabilizer of~$xy$. The determinant statement rests on the polynomial identity
$\det L_x=N(x)^4$. We prove the rank law
$\rank(\Delta-I)=2\max(0,\dim Q-2)$, first observed in a seeded census over
the split octonions modulo $\ell\in\{3,5,7,11\}$ and verified for every
pair over $\F_3$. Nondegenerate $Q$ is handled by doubling. Degenerate $Q$
follows from Moufang's theorem and the fact that every associative subalgebra
of an octonion algebra has dimension at most~$4$. The characteristic
polynomial of $\Delta$ is $(X-1)^4(X^2-\tau X+1)^2$ with
$\tau=2-N(xy-yx)/(N(x)N(y))$, and $\Delta$ is unipotent exactly when $Q$ is not a
nondegenerate quaternion algebra. Since $2N(xy-yx)$ is the Gram determinant of
$(1,x,y)$, the similarity class of $\Delta$ is classical quadratic-form data
whenever $\tau\neq\pm2$. We determine every Jordan type of $\Delta$: its
similarity class is fixed by that Gram matrix together with $\dim Q$, and
$\dim Q$ is needed. For defects of words of length four the characteristic
polynomial is not classical. We conjecture, with computational evidence, that
it is fixed by the $\operatorname{Aut}(\C)$-invariant three- and four-forms of
the letters. We then describe an
executable Lean~4 oracle for the five-term associator (Teichm\"uller) identity,
organized as a pentagon of parenthesizations. Mutation testing shows that the
closure of that identity is a weak test of an implementation: it is invariant
under a global sign change of the associator and under passage to the opposite
algebra, and only replay against an independent implementation detects the
latter. All algebraic results are elementary consequences of classical theory;
the contribution is the exact finite formulation, its structural consequences,
and a reproducible certification methodology.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Left multiplication by a nonzero element $x$ of a real division octonion
algebra is a similitude of the norm form. After normalization,
$U_x=L_x/\sqrt{N(x)}$ lies in $\SO(8)$. Because the octonions are not
associative, $x\mapsto U_x$ is not a homomorphism, and the failure is measured
by the operator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:real-defect}
  D(x,y)=U_xU_yU_{xy}^{-1}\in\SO(8),
\end{equation}
which is the identity exactly when $x(yz)=(xy)z$ for every $z$. Operators of
this kind are natural observables for word problems in the matrix group
generated by the $U_x$. Over a finite field, however, the normalization
$\sqrt{N(x)}$ need not exist, and~\eqref{eq:real-defect} is not even defined.

This paper records what survives without square roots and what can be
certified about it by exact computation. Our results are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item The square-root-free operator $\Delta(x,y)=L_xL_yL_{xy}^{-1}$ coincides
  with $D(x,y)$ over $\R$ and is defined over every field of characteristic
  $\neq2$. It is an isometry fixing $xy$, and it is trivial exactly when the
  associator $[x,y,\cdot\,]$ vanishes (Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}).
\item $\Delta(x,y)$ fixes pointwise the unital subalgebra $A$ generated by $x$
  and $y$, preserves $W^\perp$ for every subspace $W\subseteq A$, and has
  determinant~$1$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed} and
  Proposition~\ref{prop:det}). For a nondegenerate quaternion subalgebra
  $Q=\spn\{1,x,y,xy\}$ this places the defect in $\SO(Q^\perp)$.
\item The exact rank law $\rank(\Delta-I)=2\max(0,\dim Q-2)$ holds
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:rank}). For nondegenerate $Q$ it follows from doubling
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-nondeg}). For degenerate $Q$ we use Moufang's
  theorem and a bound on associative subalgebras (Lemma~\ref{lem:assoc},
  Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-four}, Proposition~\ref{prop:rank-three}). A census
  over small primes and an exhaustive computation over $\F_3$
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:f3}) check it independently. The characteristic
  polynomial of $\Delta$ depends only on $\tau=2-N(xy-yx)/(N(x)N(y))$
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:charpoly}), because the Gram determinant of
  $(1,x,y,xy)$ is $N(xy-yx)^2$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:gram}). Its Jordan type is
  fixed by the Gram matrix of $(1,x,y)$ together with $\dim Q$
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:jordan}, Corollary~\ref{cor:gram-dimq}). Word defects
  of length four see the $\operatorname{Aut}(\C)$-invariant forms of their
  letters (Proposition~\ref{prop:word-four},
  Conjecture~\ref{conj:g2}).
\item A Lean~4 oracle recomputes the five edges of the associator pentagon
  from an explicit structure tensor. A mutation analysis
  (Table~\ref{tab:mutants}) shows which checks detect which implementation
  errors, and it identifies two error classes that closure of the pentagon
  cannot detect (Observation~\ref{obs:weak}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Scope and claims}
We make no novelty claim for the algebra. Proposition~\ref{prop:basic},
Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed} and Proposition~\ref{prop:det} follow quickly from
the composition identities and Artin's theorem, and may well appear in the
literature in some form. The same holds for the rank law and for
Lemma~\ref{lem:assoc}, which rest on Moufang's theorem and the Peirce
decomposition. A dated prior-art search has not been completed (see
Section~\ref{sec:related}). The computations are finite and seeded. They
check Theorem~\ref{thm:rank} independently of its proof, and the census is
evidence, not a theorem. The Lean component is an executable checker, not a
kernel-checked proof of soundness.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

Throughout, $k$ is a field with $\operatorname{char}k\neq2$. A
\emph{composition algebra} over $k$ is a unital $k$-algebra $\C$ with a
nondegenerate quadratic form $N$ satisfying $N(xy)=N(x)N(y)$. We write
$B(x,y)=N(x+y)-N(x)-N(y)$ for the polar form, $T(x)=B(x,1)$ for the trace,
and $\conj{x}=T(x)1-x$ for the canonical involution. An \emph{octonion
algebra} is an eight-dimensional composition algebra. We use the following
classical facts~\cite{schafer1966,springer2000}.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(F\arabic*)]
\item\label{F:comp} $\C$ is alternative, $x\conj{x}=\conj{x}x=N(x)1$, and
  \[
    x(\conj{x}w)=\conj{x}(xw)=N(x)w\qquad(x,w\in\C).
  \]
  In particular $L_x$ is invertible exactly when $N(x)\neq0$, with
  $L_x^{-1}=N(x)^{-1}L_{\conj{x}}$.
\item\label{F:artin} \emph{Artin's theorem.} In an alternative algebra, the
  subalgebra generated by any two elements is associative.
\item\label{F:double} \emph{Quaternion subalgebras and doubling}
  \cite[Ch.~1]{springer2000}. Let $D\subseteq\C$ be a four-dimensional unital
  subalgebra on which $B$ is nondegenerate. Then $D$ is a quaternion algebra:
  it is associative and is either a division algebra or isomorphic to
  $M_2(k)$, with $N=\det$ in the latter case. Moreover $D^\perp$ contains an
  element $v$ with $N(v)\neq0$, $\C=D\oplus Dv$, and $a(bv)=(ba)v$ for all
  $a,b\in D$.
\item\label{F:moufang} \emph{Moufang}~\cite[Ch.~III]{schafer1966}. Every
  alternative algebra satisfies the left Moufang identity
  $x(y(xz))=(xyx)z$. If $\assoc xyz=0$, then $x$, $y$ and $z$ generate an
  associative subalgebra (Moufang's theorem).
\item\label{F:zorn} \emph{Peirce decomposition}
  \cite{zorn1933}, \cite[Ch.~1]{springer2000}. If $e\in\C$ is an idempotent
  with $e\notin\{0,1\}$, there is an isomorphism of $\C$ onto Zorn's
  vector-matrix algebra taking $e$ to $\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$. Under it,
  $\C_{10}=\{u:eu=u,\ ue=0\}$ is the space of matrices whose only nonzero
  entry is the upper vector $u\in k^3$, and the product of two such matrices
  is the matrix whose only nonzero entry is the lower vector $\pm u\times u'$.
  Hence $\dim\C_{10}=3$, $e(uu')=0$ for $u,u'\in\C_{10}$, and $uu'=0$ only if
  $u$ and $u'$ are linearly dependent.
\end{enumerate}

The associator is $\assoc{x}{y}{z}=x(yz)-(xy)z$. It is trilinear and vanishes
whenever one argument is~$1$.

\subsection*{The finite model}
Let $\ell$ be an odd prime. Iterating the Cayley--Dickson doubling
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cd}
  (a,b)(c,d)=(ac+\gamma\,\conj{d}b,\ da+b\conj{c}),\qquad
  \conj{(a,b)}=(\conj{a},-b),
\end{equation}
three times over $\F_\ell$ with $\gamma=-1$ at each stage gives an octonion
algebra $\C_\ell$ on $\F_\ell^8$ with $N(x)=\sum_i x_i^2$. The diagonal form
in eight variables is isotropic over $\F_\ell$ by the Chevalley--Warning
theorem~\cite{chevalley1935,warning1935}. Hence $\C_\ell$ is the split
octonion algebra, which is unique up to isomorphism (Zorn's vector-matrix
algebra~\cite{zorn1933}; see also~\cite[Ch.~1]{springer2000}). Our
computations use this model; the theory of Section~\ref{sec:defect} applies to
every octonion algebra over every field of characteristic $\neq2$.

\section{The square-root-free defect}\label{sec:defect}

\begin{definition}
For $x,y\in\C$ with $N(x)N(y)\neq0$, set
\[
  \Delta(x,y)=L_xL_yL_{xy}^{-1}
  =\bigl(N(x)N(y)\bigr)^{-1}L_xL_yL_{\conj{xy}}.
\]
The second expression follows from~\ref{F:comp}, since
$N(xy)=N(x)N(y)\neq0$. Set $A=A(x,y)$ for the unital subalgebra generated by
$x$ and $y$, and $Q=Q(x,y)=\spn\{1,x,y,xy\}\subseteq A$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:real}
For $k=\R$ and $\C$ the division octonions,
$U_xU_yU_{xy}^{-1}=\frac{\sqrt{N(xy)}}{\sqrt{N(x)}\sqrt{N(y)}}\,\Delta(x,y)
=\Delta(x,y)$. Thus $\Delta$ extends~\eqref{eq:real-defect}, and it uses only
field operations.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:basic}
Let $N(x)N(y)\neq0$ and $\Delta=\Delta(x,y)$. Then
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $\Delta\in\Ort(\C,N)$;
\item $\Delta(xy)=xy$;
\item $\Delta=I$ if and only if $\assoc{x}{y}{z}=0$ for every $z\in\C$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) By composition, $N(L_av)=N(a)N(v)$ for all $a,v$. Applying this to
$v=L_{xy}^{-1}u$ gives $N(u)=N(xy)\,N(L_{xy}^{-1}u)$. Hence
$N(\Delta u)=N(x)N(y)N(xy)^{-1}N(u)=N(u)$, and polarization gives
invariance of~$B$.
(b) $L_{xy}^{-1}(xy)=1$, so $\Delta(xy)=x(y\cdot1)=xy$.
(c) $\Delta=I$ if and only if $L_xL_y=L_{xy}$, and evaluating at every $z$ gives
$x(yz)=(xy)z$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:fixed}
Let $N(x)N(y)\neq0$ and $\Delta=\Delta(x,y)$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $\Delta w=w$ for every $w\in A$, and in particular for every $w\in Q$;
\item $\Delta(W^\perp)=W^\perp$ for every subspace $W\subseteq A$;
\item if $B|_W$ is nondegenerate for a subspace $W\subseteq A$, then
  $\C=W\oplus W^\perp$ and $\Delta=\mathrm{id}_W\oplus\Delta|_{W^\perp}$ with
  $\Delta|_{W^\perp}\in\Ort(W^\perp,N)$;
\item $\rank(\Delta-I)\le 8-\dim A\le 8-\dim Q$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(a) Let $A_0$ be the subalgebra generated by $x$ and $y$ without the unit. By
\ref{F:comp} and \ref{F:artin}, $A_0$ is associative. Every element of $A$ has
the form $\alpha1+a_0$ with $a_0\in A_0$. Since the associator is trilinear and
vanishes when an argument is $1$, it vanishes on $A$, so $A$ is associative.
Let $w\in A$ and put $z=N(xy)^{-1}\conj{xy}\,w$. Since
$\conj{xy}=T(xy)1-xy\in A$, we have $z\in A$, and
$z=L_{xy}^{-1}w$ by~\ref{F:comp}. Associativity of $A$ gives
\[
  \Delta w=x(yz)=(xy)z=L_{xy}L_{xy}^{-1}w=w.
\]
(b) Let $u\in W^\perp$ and $w\in W$. By (a) and
Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(a),
$B(\Delta u,w)=B(\Delta u,\Delta w)=B(u,w)=0$, so
$\Delta(W^\perp)\subseteq W^\perp$. Equality holds because $\Delta$ is injective
and $W^\perp$ is finite-dimensional.
(c) Nondegeneracy of $B|_W$ gives $W\cap W^\perp=0$ and
$\dim W^\perp=8-\dim W$. The rest follows from (a), (b) and
Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(a).
(d) By (a), $A\subseteq\ker(\Delta-I)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:det}
For every $x\in\C$, $\det L_x=N(x)^4$. Consequently $\det\Delta(x,y)=1$
whenever $N(x)N(y)\neq0$, so $\Delta(x,y)\in\SO(\C,N)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix a basis $e_0,\dots,e_7$ of $\C$, let $R=k[t_0,\dots,t_7]$, and let
$\xi=\sum_it_ie_i\in\C_R=\C\otimes_kR$. The entries of $L_\xi$ and
$L_{\conj\xi}$ are linear forms in the $t_i$. Expanding,
\[
  \conj\xi(\xi w)
  =\sum_it_i^2\,\conj{e_i}(e_iw)
   +\sum_{i<j}t_it_j\bigl(\conj{e_i}(e_jw)+\conj{e_j}(e_iw)\bigr).
\]
By~\ref{F:comp} and its polarization in $\C$, the right-hand side is
$\bigl(\sum_it_i^2N(e_i)+\sum_{i<j}t_it_jB(e_i,e_j)\bigr)w=N(\xi)w$. Hence
$L_{\conj\xi}L_\xi=N(\xi)I$ over $R$, and
\[
  \det L_{\conj\xi}\cdot\det L_\xi=N(\xi)^8\quad\text{in }R.
\]
A nondegenerate quadratic form in at least three variables is irreducible: a
product of two linear forms has rank at most~$2$. Since $R$ is a unique
factorization domain, $\det L_\xi=c\,N(\xi)^a$ with $c\in k^\times$. As
$\det L_\xi$ is homogeneous of degree $8$, we get $a=4$, and specializing
$\xi\mapsto1$ gives $c=\det I=1$. Specializing $t$ gives $\det L_x=N(x)^4$ for
every $x\in\C$. Finally,
\[
  \det\Delta(x,y)=\bigl(N(x)N(y)\bigr)^{-8}\,N(x)^4N(y)^4N(\conj{xy})^4=1,
\]
since $N(\conj{xy})=N(xy)=N(x)N(y)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:so4}
If $\dim Q=4$ and $B|_Q$ is nondegenerate, then
$\Delta(x,y)=\mathrm{id}_Q\oplus R$ with $R\in\SO(Q^\perp,N)$ and $\rank(\Delta-I)\le4$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed}(c) with $W=Q$, $R=\Delta|_{Q^\perp}$ is an isometry.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:det}, $\det R=\det\Delta=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:basic} places the defect in the stabilizer of $xy$, a
copy of $\Ort(7)$. Corollary~\ref{cor:so4} shrinks this to the pointwise
stabilizer of a four-dimensional subspace, a copy of $\SO(4)$. Over
$\F_\ell$ the latter is a group of order $O(\ell^6)$, compared with
$O(\ell^{21})$ for the former.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[rank law]\label{thm:rank}
Let $x,y\in\C$ and $\varphi_{x,y}(z)=\assoc xyz$. Then
\[
  \rank\varphi_{x,y}=2\max(0,\dim Q-2)=
  \begin{cases}0,&\dim Q\le2,\\ 2,&\dim Q=3,\\ 4,&\dim Q=4,\end{cases}
\]
and if $\dim Q=4$, then $\ker\varphi_{x,y}=Q$. If $N(x)N(y)\neq0$, then
$\rank(\Delta(x,y)-I)=\rank\varphi_{x,y}$.
\end{theorem}

The census of Section~\ref{sec:census} suggested this law, and over $\F_3$ it
was first verified by exhaustion (Proposition~\ref{prop:f3}). The proof
occupies the rest of this section. The case $\dim Q\le2$ is
Proposition~\ref{prop:flat}. For nondegenerate $B|_Q$ a doubling argument
applies (Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-nondeg}). This leaves the cases where $B|_Q$ is
degenerate, which include every case with $\dim Q=3$, since a nondegenerate
unital subalgebra of $\C$ is a composition algebra, and composition algebras
have dimension $1$, $2$, $4$ or $8$ \cite{hurwitz1898,springer2000}. For
these cases $\C$ does not split as $Q\oplus Q^\perp$. They follow instead from
Moufang's theorem and a bound on associative subalgebras
(Lemma~\ref{lem:assoc}, Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-four} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:rank-three}).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:flat}
If $\dim Q\le2$, then $\Delta(x,y)=I$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $\dim Q\le2$, then $1,x,y$ are linearly dependent. If $x\in k1$, then $L_x$ is
scalar and $\assoc xyz=0$ for every $z$. Otherwise $y=\alpha1+\beta x$ for some
$\alpha,\beta\in k$, and
$\assoc xyz=\beta\assoc xxz=0$ by alternativity~\ref{F:comp}. In either case
$\Delta=I$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(c).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:phi}
Put $\varphi=\varphi_{x,y}$, $\varphi(z)=\assoc xyz$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $Q$ is closed under multiplication, so $Q=A$.
\item If $N(x)N(y)\neq0$, then $\Delta w=w$ if and only if
  $L_{xy}^{-1}w\in\ker\varphi$. Hence $\rank(\Delta-I)=\rank\varphi$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) By~\ref{F:comp}, every $a\in\C$ satisfies $a^2-T(a)a+N(a)1=0$, and
linearizing gives $ab+ba=T(a)b+T(b)a-B(a,b)1$. These identities place $x^2$,
$y^2$, $(xy)^2$ and $yx$ in $Q$. By alternativity, $x(xy)=x^2y$ and
$(xy)y=xy^2$, and both lie in $Q$. The linearized identity, applied to the
pairs $(xy,x)$ and $(y,xy)$, then places $(xy)x$ and $y(xy)$ in $Q$.
(b) $\Delta w=w$ means $x(yz)=(xy)z$ for $z=L_{xy}^{-1}w$. So $\ker(\Delta-I)$ is
the image of $\ker\varphi$ under the invertible map $L_{xy}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:m2}
Let $a,b\in M_2(k)$, $\operatorname{char}k\neq2$, with $\det(ab-ba)=0$.
Then the unital subalgebra generated by $a$ and $b$ has dimension at most~$3$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Subtracting scalar multiples of $I$ changes neither the commutator
$c=ab-ba$ nor the generated algebra, so we may assume $\tr a=\tr b=0$. By the
Cayley--Hamilton theorem a trace-zero $2\times2$ matrix squares to a scalar.
Hence $ab+ba=(a+b)^2-a^2-b^2$ is scalar, $c$ has trace zero, and
$c^2=-\det(c)I=0$. Since $a^2$ is scalar, $ac+ca=a^2b-ba^2=0$, and likewise
$bc+cb=0$. If $c=0$, then $a$ and $b$ commute. A non-scalar $2\times2$ matrix has
a centralizer of dimension~$2$, so the generated algebra has dimension at
most~$2$. If $c\neq0$, then $L=\ker c$ is a line, and for $w\in L$ we have
$c(aw)=-a(cw)=0$, so $aL\subseteq L$, and likewise $bL\subseteq L$. The
generated algebra therefore stabilizes the line $L$, and the stabilizer of a
line in $M_2(k)$ has dimension~$3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[rank law, nondegenerate case]\label{thm:rank-nondeg}
Suppose $\dim Q=4$ and $B|_Q$ is nondegenerate. Then $\ker\varphi_{x,y}=Q$.
In particular, if $N(x)N(y)\neq0$, then $\rank(\Delta(x,y)-I)=4$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:phi}(a) and~\ref{F:double}, $Q$ is an associative quaternion
algebra, and $\C=Q\oplus Qv$ with $v\in Q^\perp$ and $N(v)\neq0$. Put
$c=yx-xy\in Q$. For $q,u\in Q$, associativity of $Q$ gives
$\assoc xyq=0$, and the identity $a(bv)=(ba)v$ gives
\[
  \assoc{x}{y}{uv}=x\bigl((uy)v\bigr)-\bigl(u(xy)\bigr)v
  =\bigl((uy)x\bigr)v-\bigl(u(xy)\bigr)v=(uc)v.
\]
Thus $\varphi(q+uv)=(uc)v$. Applying the involution, $(uc)v=0$ if and only if
$\conj v\,\conj{uc}=0$, which holds if and only if $uc=0$ by~\ref{F:comp},
since $N(\conj v)=N(v)\neq0$. Hence $\ker\varphi=Q\oplus\{uv:uc=0\}$. It
remains to show that $N(c)\neq0$. Then $uc=0$ gives $\conj c\,\conj u=0$, and
so $u=0$, again by~\ref{F:comp}. First, $c\neq0$: otherwise $Q$, which is
generated by $x$ and $y$, would be commutative, and a quaternion algebra is
not commutative. If $N(c)=0$, then $Q$ is not a division algebra, so
$Q\cong M_2(k)$ with $N=\det$ by~\ref{F:double}. Then $\det c=0$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:m2} gives $\dim Q\le3$, a contradiction. The last statement
follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:phi}(b).
\end{proof}

We turn to degenerate $Q$. Three lemmas come first. Besides~\ref{F:comp} we
use the standard consequences $B(ab,c)=B(b,\conj ac)=B(a,c\conj b)$,
$a\conj b+b\conj a=B(a,b)1$, and the polarization
$B(ab,dc)+B(db,ac)=B(a,d)B(b,c)$ of $N(ab)=N(a)N(b)$. An element $a$ is
\emph{pure} if $T(a)=0$. Pure elements $a,b$ satisfy $a^2=-N(a)1$ and
$ab+ba=-B(a,b)1$, by the identities in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:phi}(a).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:skew}
For all $x,y,z,w\in\C$, $B(\assoc xyz,w)=-B(z,\assoc xyw)$. Hence
$\varphi_{x,y}$ is skew-adjoint for $B$, and $\rank\varphi_{x,y}$ is even.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By the identities above, $B(x(yz),w)=B(yz,\conj xw)$ and
$B((xy)z,w)=B(xy,w\conj z)$. Therefore
\[
  B(\assoc xyz,w)+B(\assoc xyw,z)
  =B(yz,\conj xw)+B(yw,\conj xz)-B\bigl(xy,\,w\conj z+z\conj w\bigr).
\]
The polarization with $(a,b,d,c)=(y,z,\conj x,w)$ gives
$B(yz,\conj xw)+B(\conj xz,yw)=B(y,\conj x)B(z,w)$. Also
$w\conj z+z\conj w=B(z,w)1$ and $B(xy,1)=B(y,\conj x)$. The right-hand side is
therefore $0$. In a basis, skew-adjointness says that $GM$ is skew-symmetric,
where $G$ is the Gram matrix of $B$ and $M$ the matrix of $\varphi_{x,y}$. A
skew-symmetric matrix has even rank in characteristic $\neq2$, and
$\rank M=\rank GM$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:iso}
Let $n\in\C$ be nonzero and pure with $N(n)=0$. Then $L_n^2=0$, and
$n\C=\ker L_n$ is a totally isotropic subspace of dimension~$4$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $n^2=-N(n)1=0$, alternativity gives $L_n^2=L_{n^2}=0$, so
$n\C\subseteq\ker L_n$ and $\rank L_n\le4$. The trace-zero subspace
$1^\perp$ is nondegenerate, because $N(1)=1\neq0$. It contains $n$, so it
contains some $m'$ with $B(n,m')=1$. Then $m=m'-N(m')n$ is pure, $N(m)=0$ and
$B(n,m)=1$. Linearizing $a(az)=a^2z$ gives
$L_nL_m+L_mL_n=L_{nm+mn}=-B(n,m)I=-I$, so $\C=n\C+m\C$ and
$\rank L_n+\rank L_m\ge8$. Since $m$ also satisfies the hypotheses,
$\rank L_m\le4$. Hence $\rank L_n=4$ and $n\C=\ker L_n$. Finally,
$N(nc)=N(n)N(c)=0$ for every $c$, and polarizing shows that $n\C$ is totally
isotropic.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:assoc}
Every unital associative subalgebra $S$ of $\C$ satisfies $\dim S\le4$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $R=S\cap S^\perp$ and $r=\dim R$.

\emph{Structure of $R$.} For $s,w\in S$ and $n\in R$, we have
$B(sn,w)=B(n,\conj sw)=0$ and $B(ns,w)=B(n,w\conj s)=0$, because
$\conj s=T(s)1-s\in S$. So $R$ is an ideal of $S$. As $1\in S$, $R$ is a
totally isotropic subspace of the seven-dimensional nondegenerate space
$1^\perp$, so $r\le3$. Every $n\in R$ is pure with $N(n)=0$.

\emph{The quotient.} $N(s+n)=N(s)+B(s,n)+N(n)=N(s)$ for $s\in S$ and $n\in R$, so
$N$ induces a nondegenerate multiplicative form on $\bar S=S/R$. Thus $\bar S$
is an associative composition algebra, and $\dim\bar S\in\{1,2,4\}$
\cite{hurwitz1898,springer2000}. We show $\dim S\le4$ in each case.

\emph{Case $\dim\bar S=1$.} Then $\dim S=1+r\le4$.

\emph{Case $\dim\bar S=4$.} A four-dimensional composition algebra is the
Cayley--Dickson double of a two-dimensional one \cite[Ch.~1]{springer2000},
so it has a basis $1,i,j,ij$ and is generated by two elements. Lift them to $x,y\in S$. Then $Q=Q(x,y)$ is a subalgebra
(Lemma~\ref{lem:phi}(a)) mapping onto $\bar S$, so $\dim Q=4$ and
$Q\to\bar S$ is a bijection. Since $R\perp S$, it is an isometry, so $B|_Q$ is
nondegenerate. By Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-nondeg}, $\ker\varphi_{x,y}=Q$. As $S$
is associative, $S\subseteq\ker\varphi_{x,y}=Q$, so $\dim S=4$.

\emph{Case $\dim\bar S=2$.} Here $\dim S=2+r$, and we show $r\neq3$. Suppose
$r=3$. Choose a pure $t\in S\setminus R$; then $N(t)\neq0$, because $\bar S$ is
nondegenerate and $\bar t\perp1$. For $n\in R$ we have $tn\in R$,
$t(tn)=t^2n=-N(t)n$, and $nt=-tn$, since $B(n,t)=0$. Put $c=-N(t)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item If $c$ is not a square in $k$, then $L_t|_R$ makes $R$ a vector space
  over the field $k(\sqrt c)$, so $r$ is even.
\item If $c=\lambda^2$, then $\lambda\neq0$, and $R=R_+\oplus R_-$ with
  $R_\pm=\{n\in R:tn=\pm\lambda n\}$. Replacing $t$ by $-t$ if necessary,
  $\dim R_+\ge2$. The element $e=\tfrac12(1+\lambda^{-1}t)\in S$ is an
  idempotent other than $0$ and $1$. For $n\in R_+$ we have $en=n$ and
  $ne=\tfrac12(n-\lambda^{-1}tn)=0$, so $R_+\subseteq\C_{10}$. For
  $n,m\in R_+$, associativity of $S$ gives $e(nm)=(en)m=nm$, while
  $e(nm)=0$ by~\ref{F:zorn}. Hence $nm=0$, and by~\ref{F:zorn} $n$ and $m$
  are linearly dependent. This contradicts $\dim R_+\ge2$.
\end{enumerate}
In both subcases $r\neq3$, so $r\le2$ and $\dim S\le4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[rank law, $\dim Q=4$]\label{thm:rank-four}
If $\dim Q=4$, then $\ker\varphi_{x,y}=Q$, whether or not $B|_Q$ is
nondegenerate.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$Q=A$ is associative (Lemma~\ref{lem:phi}(a) and Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed}), so
$Q\subseteq\ker\varphi_{x,y}$. Conversely, let $\assoc xyz=0$. By Moufang's
theorem~\ref{F:moufang}, $x$, $y$ and $z$ generate an associative subalgebra,
and adjoining $1$ preserves associativity. The result is a unital
associative subalgebra containing $Q+kz$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:assoc} it has
dimension at most $4=\dim Q$, so $z\in Q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[rank law, $\dim Q=3$]\label{prop:rank-three}
If $\dim Q=3$, then $\rank\varphi_{x,y}=2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
As in Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-four}, $Q=A$ is associative and
$Q\subseteq\ker\varphi_{x,y}$. By the discussion after Theorem~\ref{thm:rank},
$B|_Q$ is degenerate, so $R_Q=Q\cap Q^\perp\neq0$. As in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:assoc}, $R_Q$ is an ideal of $Q$ consisting of pure elements of
norm $0$. Let $Q_0$ be the trace-zero part of $Q$, so $Q=k1\oplus Q_0$,
$\dim Q_0=2$, and $R_Q\subseteq Q_0$.

\emph{A convenient basis.} The associator is trilinear, alternating, and
vanishes on scalars. So for every basis $(n,t)$ of $Q_0$,
$\varphi_{x,y}=\mu\varphi_{n,t}$ with $\mu\in k^\times$. We choose
$0\neq n\in R_Q$ with $nt\in kn$. If $\dim R_Q=1$, any $n\in R_Q$ works, since
$R_Q$ is an ideal. If $\dim R_Q=2$, then $R_Q=Q_0$. Pure elements of norm $0$
that are orthogonal anticommute and square to $0$, so for a basis $n_1,n_2$ of
$Q_0$ the products of elements of $Q_0$ lie in $kn_1n_2$. If $n_1n_2=0$, take
$n=n_1$. Otherwise take $n=n_1n_2\in Q_0$. By associativity of $Q$,
$nn_1=-n_1^2n_2=0$ and $nn_2=n_1n_2^2=0$, so $nt=0$ for every $t$.

\emph{Five kernel dimensions.} Write $nt=\alpha n$. For $c\in\C$, the left
Moufang identity~\ref{F:moufang} and $n^2=0$ give
\[
  \assoc nt{nc}=n(t(nc))-(nt)(nc)=(ntn)c-\alpha n(nc)
  =\alpha n^2c-\alpha n^2c=0.
\]
So $n\C\subseteq\ker\varphi_{n,t}=\ker\varphi_{x,y}$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:iso}, $n\C$ is totally isotropic of dimension $4$. The space
$Q\cap n\C$ is a totally isotropic subspace of $Q$. Its image in the
nondegenerate space $Q/R_Q$ is totally isotropic, of dimension at most
$\lfloor(3-\dim R_Q)/2\rfloor$. Hence
$\dim(Q\cap n\C)\le\dim R_Q+\lfloor(3-\dim R_Q)/2\rfloor=2$, and
$\dim\ker\varphi_{x,y}\ge\dim(Q+n\C)\ge3+4-2=5$, so
$\rank\varphi_{x,y}\le3$, and $\rank\varphi_{x,y}\le2$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:skew}.

\emph{$\varphi_{x,y}\neq0$.} Suppose $\assoc ntc=0$ for every $c\in\C$. By
Moufang's theorem and Lemma~\ref{lem:assoc}, for $c\notin Q$ the unital
subalgebra generated by $n$, $t$ and $c$ has dimension at most $4$. It
contains $Q+kc$, so it equals $Q\oplus kc$, and $nc\in Q+kc$. Since
$nQ\subseteq Q$, $L_n$ induces an endomorphism of the five-dimensional space
$\C/Q$ for which every vector is an eigenvector. Such an endomorphism is a
scalar $\lambda$, and $\lambda^2=0$ because $L_n^2=0$. Then
$n\C\subseteq Q$, contradicting $\dim n\C=4>3$. So $\rank\varphi_{x,y}>0$,
and being even it equals~$2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:rank}]
Combine Proposition~\ref{prop:flat}, whose proof shows $\varphi_{x,y}=0$,
Proposition~\ref{prop:rank-three} and Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-four}. The last
statement is Lemma~\ref{lem:phi}(b).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-four} contains Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-nondeg}, but its
proof uses Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-nondeg} in the case $\dim\bar S=4$ of
Lemma~\ref{lem:assoc}. Case~(b) of that lemma arises only when $\C$ has a
nontrivial idempotent, that is, when $\C$ is split. In a division octonion
algebra $Q$ is never degenerate, since $\C$ has no nonzero isotropic vectors.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{The characteristic polynomial}
The rank law leaves open which conjugacy class $\Delta$ lies in. A single
scalar fixes its characteristic polynomial. Throughout, $c=xy-yx$ and
$\nu=N(x)N(y)$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:gram}
For all $x,y\in\C$, the Gram determinant of $(1,x,y,xy)$ with respect to $B$
equals $N(c)^2$. Hence $N(c)=0$ if $\dim Q\le3$. If $\dim Q=4$, then $B|_Q$ is
nondegenerate if and only if $N(c)\neq0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Both sides are polynomial functions of $(x,y)$, and they are unchanged by
extending scalars to an algebraic closure. It therefore suffices to prove the
identity on a Zariski-dense set of pairs over an infinite field. Replacing
$x,y$ by $x-\alpha1$, $y-\beta1$, or $y$ by $y-\lambda x$, changes neither $c$
nor the Gram determinant: $(1,x,y,xy)$ changes by a unitriangular matrix. So
we may assume that $x$ and $y$ are pure and, when $N(x)\neq0$, that $B(x,y)=0$.
Then $xy=-yx$ is pure and orthogonal to $x$ and $y$, since
$B(xy,x)=N(x)B(y,1)=0$ and $B(xy,y)=N(y)B(x,1)=0$. The Gram matrix is
$\operatorname{diag}(2,2N(x),2N(y),2N(x)N(y))$, with determinant
$16N(x)^2N(y)^2$. Also $c=2xy$, so $N(c)^2=16N(x)^2N(y)^2$. The pairs whose
$x$ has a trace-zero part of nonzero norm form a dense set, since $N$ is not
identically zero on the nondegenerate space $1^\perp$. The last two statements follow because four linearly
dependent vectors have Gram determinant $0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:charpoly}
For all $x,y\in\C$, in the polynomial ring $k[Y]$,
\[
  \det\bigl(YI-L_xL_yL_{\conj{xy}}\bigr)
  =(Y-\nu)^4\bigl(Y^2-(2\nu-N(c))Y+\nu^2\bigr)^2.
\]
Consequently, if $\nu\neq0$ and $\tau=2-N(c)/\nu$, then
$\det(XI-\Delta(x,y))=(X-1)^4(X^2-\tau X+1)^2$ and
$\tr\Delta(x,y)=8-2N(c)/\nu$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
As in Lemma~\ref{lem:gram}, it suffices to prove the identity on the dense set
where $\nu N(c)\neq0$. This set is nonempty, because $\C$ contains a
quaternion subalgebra with an orthogonal basis $1,i,j,ij$ of nonzero norms
\cite[Ch.~1]{springer2000}, and $N(ij-ji)=4N(i)N(j)$. There, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:gram}, $Q$ is a nondegenerate quaternion subalgebra, and
$\C=Q\oplus Qv$ with $a(bv)=(ba)v$ for $a,b\in Q$ by~\ref{F:double}. Let
$M=L_xL_yL_{\conj{xy}}=\nu\Delta(x,y)$. On $Q$, associativity gives
$M(w)=x(y(\conj{xy}w))=((xy)\conj{xy})w=\nu w$ for every $w\in Q$.
For $u\in Q$,
\[
  M(uv)=\bigl(u\,\conj{xy}\,yx\bigr)v=(uh)v,\qquad h=\conj{xy}\,yx\in Q,
\]
so on $Qv\cong Q$, $M$ is right multiplication by $h$. After extending scalars,
$Q\cong M_2$ with $N=\det$. Right multiplication by $h$ then acts on each row
of a $2\times2$ matrix by $h$, so its characteristic polynomial is
$(Y^2-T(h)Y+N(h))^2$. Here $N(h)=\nu^2$ and
$T(h)=B(xy,yx)=N(xy)+N(yx)-N(c)=2\nu-N(c)$. The second statement follows on
dividing by $\nu^8$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:class}
Let $\nu\neq0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item If $N(c)\neq0$, then $Q$ is a nondegenerate quaternion subalgebra, and
  $\Delta=\mathrm{id}_Q\oplus\rho$ with $\rho(uv)=(ug)v$ and
  $g=(xy)^{-1}yx$, a norm-one element of $Q$ with $T(g)=\tau\neq2$.
\item If $N(c)=0$, then $\Delta$ is unipotent, and $\rank(\Delta-I)$ is
  given by Theorem~\ref{thm:rank}. This is the case exactly when
  $\dim Q\le3$ or $B|_Q$ is degenerate.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
(a) Divide $M(uv)=(uh)v$ by $\nu$ and use $(xy)^{-1}=\nu^{-1}\conj{xy}$.
Then $T(g)=\tau$ and $N(g)=1$. (b) If $N(c)=0$, then $\tau=2$ and the
characteristic polynomial is $(X-1)^8$, so Cayley--Hamilton gives
$(\Delta-I)^8=0$. Conversely, if $N(c)\neq0$, the quadratic factor at
$X=1$ is $2-\tau=N(c)/\nu\neq0$, so the characteristic polynomial
cannot be $(X-1)^8$ and $\Delta$ is not unipotent. The dimensional
equivalence is Lemma~\ref{lem:gram}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:classical}
$2N(xy-yx)=\det\bigl(B(u,w)\bigr)_{u,w\in\{1,x,y\}}$. Hence, for $\nu\neq0$,
\[
  \tau=2-\frac{\det\bigl(B(u,w)\bigr)_{u,w\in\{1,x,y\}}}{2N(x)N(y)},
\]
and the characteristic polynomial of $\Delta(x,y)$ depends only on the Gram
matrix of $(1,x,y)$. If $\tau\neq\pm2$, then $\Delta(x,y)$ is semisimple, so
its similarity class in $\mathrm{GL}(\C)$ depends only on that Gram matrix.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $x=\tfrac12T(x)1+x_0$ and $y=\tfrac12T(y)1+y_0$ with $x_0,y_0$ pure.
Then $xy-yx=x_0y_0-y_0x_0=2x_0y_0+B(x_0,y_0)1$, and
$N(xy-yx)=4N(x_0)N(y_0)+2B(x_0,y_0)B(x_0y_0,1)+B(x_0,y_0)^2
=4N(x_0)N(y_0)-B(x_0,y_0)^2$, because $B(x_0y_0,1)=B(y_0,\conj{x_0})=-B(x_0,y_0)$.
This is the Gram determinant of $(x_0,y_0)$. Since $B(1,1)=2$ and $x_0,y_0$ are
the projections of $x,y$ to $1^\perp$, the Gram determinant of $(1,x,y)$ is
twice it. If $\tau\neq\pm2$, the polynomial $(X-1)(X^2-\tau X+1)$ has
distinct roots, and it annihilates $\Delta$: on $Q$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed}(a), and on $Qv$ because $g^2-\tau g+1=0$ in $Q$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:class}(a); note $\tau\neq2$ forces $N(c)\neq0$). A
matrix annihilated by a polynomial with distinct roots is semisimple, and a
semisimple matrix is determined up to similarity by its characteristic
polynomial.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:reflections}
Let $\nu N(c)\neq0$, and let $v\in Q^\perp$ with $N(v)\neq0$. Write $s_a$ for the
reflection $z\mapsto z-N(a)^{-1}B(z,a)a$. Then
\[
  \Delta(x,y)=s_{xv}\,s_v\,s_{yv}\,s_{(xy)v}.
\]
In particular, the spinor norm of $\Delta(x,y)$ is
$N(xv)N(v)N(yv)N((xy)v)\,k^{\times2}=N(v)^4\nu^2\,k^{\times2}$, which is trivial.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:gram}, $Q$ is a nondegenerate quaternion subalgebra, and
$\C=Q\oplus Qv$ as in~\ref{F:double}. For $a\in Q$ with $N(a)\neq0$, the
reflection $s_{av}$ fixes $Q$ pointwise. Since $u\mapsto uv$ is a similitude
with multiplier $N(v)$, $B(uv,av)=N(v)B(u,a)$, so $s_{av}(uv)=(s_au)v$, where
on the right $s_a$ is the reflection of $(Q,N)$. In the associative algebra
$Q$, $s_a(u)=-a\conj u a/N(a)$, and so
$s_as_b(u)=(a\conj b)\,u\,(\conj ba)/(N(a)N(b))$. Taking $(a,b)=(x,1)$ and
then $(y,xy)$, and using $y\,\conj{xy}=N(y)\conj x$,
\[
  s_xs_1s_ys_{xy}(u)=\frac{x\,(N(y)\conj x)\,u\,(\conj{xy}\,yx)}{N(x)\,N(y)\,\nu}
  =u\,\frac{\conj{xy}\,yx}{\nu}=ug,
\]
with $g=(xy)^{-1}yx$. By Corollary~\ref{cor:class}(a) this is $\Delta$ on
$Qv$, and both sides are the identity on $Q$. The spinor norm of a product of
reflections $s_{a_1}\cdots s_{a_r}$ is $\prod N(a_i)$ modulo squares, and
$N(xv)N(v)N(yv)N((xy)v)=N(v)^4N(x)^2N(y)^2$.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:reflections} also exhibits $g$ as the group commutator
$y^{-1}x^{-1}yx$ in $Q^\times$, and $\Delta|_{Qv}$ as the image of that
commutator under right multiplication.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:so-class}
Let $k=\F_q$, $q$ odd, and $\nu\neq0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item The spinor norm of $\Delta(x,y)$ is trivial.
\item If $N(c)\neq0$ and $\Delta(x,y)$ is semisimple (by
  Corollary~\ref{cor:classical}, this holds whenever $\tau\neq-2$, and for
  $\tau=-2$ exactly when $T(x)=T(y)=B(x,y)=0$, by
  Theorem~\ref{thm:jordan}(b)), then the
  conjugacy class of $\Delta(x,y)$ in $\SO(\C,N)$ depends only on $\tau$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
(a) If $N(c)\neq0$, this is Theorem~\ref{thm:reflections}. Otherwise
$\Delta$ is unipotent by Corollary~\ref{cor:class}(b), so its order is a
power of the odd characteristic, and its image under the spinor norm, a
homomorphism to a group of order~$2$, is trivial.
(b) By Wall's classification~\cite{wall1963}, two semisimple elements of
$\Ort(\C,N)$ over $\F_q$ are conjugate in $\Ort(\C,N)$ if and only if they
have the same characteristic polynomial and the restrictions of $N$ to their
$(+1)$- and $(-1)$-eigenspaces are isometric. Here the characteristic
polynomial is fixed by $\tau$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:charpoly}). The
$(+1)$-eigenspace is exactly $Q$, because $N(g-1)=N(c)/\nu\neq0$. Its Gram
determinant $N(c)^2$ is a nonzero square, and over $\F_q$ a nondegenerate
quadratic space is determined up to isometry by its dimension and
discriminant. If $\tau\neq-2$, there is no eigenvalue $-1$. If $\tau=-2$ and
$\Delta$ is semisimple, the $(-1)$-eigenspace is $Q^\perp$, whose isometry
class is then fixed by Witt cancellation. So any two such defects with the same
$\tau$ are conjugate in $\Ort(\C,N)$. Finally, the reflection $s_1$ has
determinant $-1$ and commutes with $\Delta$: both preserve $Q$ and $Q^\perp$,
$\Delta$ is the identity on $Q$, and $s_1$ is the identity on
$Q^\perp\subseteq1^\perp$. Hence $\Ort$-conjugacy implies $\SO$-conjugacy.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:unipotent}
The hypothesis that $\Delta$ is semisimple cannot be dropped. Over $\F_3$, the
pairs $x=(1,0,2,1,0,1,0,0)$, $y=(0,1,2,2,0,0,2,1)$ and
$x'=(1,2,2,2,1,2,1,0)$, $y'=(0,2,0,0,2,1,1,1)$ have $N(x)=N(x')=1$,
$N(y)=N(y')=2$, the same Gram matrix of $(1,x,y)$, and $N(c)=0$, so both
defects are unipotent. But $\dim Q=4$ for the first pair and $\dim Q=3$ for
the second, so $\rank(\Delta-I)$ is $4$ and $2$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:rank}, and
the two defects are not similar. When $B|_Q$ is degenerate, whether $xy$ lies
in $\spn\{1,x,y\}$ is not determined by the Gram matrix of $(1,x,y)$. By
Corollary~\ref{cor:gram-dimq}, $\dim Q$ is the only such datum.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Defects of words}
For letters $a_1,\dots,a_n\in\C$ of nonzero norm, let $v=(\cdots(a_1a_2)\cdots)a_n$
and define the \emph{word defect}
$W(a_1,\dots,a_n)=L_{a_1}\cdots L_{a_n}L_v^{-1}$. Thus $W(a,b)=\Delta(a,b)$ and
$W(a,b,c)=\Delta(a,b)\Delta(ab,c)$. We call a function of the letters
\emph{classical} if it depends only on the Gram matrix of $(1,a_1,\dots,a_n)$,
that is, on the orbit data of the letters under the stabilizer of $1$ in
$\Ort(\C,N)$. Corollaries~\ref{cor:classical} and~\ref{cor:so-class} say that
the characteristic polynomial of a single defect is classical, and so is its
class when it is semisimple. Non-semisimple single defects are not classical
(Remark~\ref{rem:unipotent}). For words, the characteristic polynomial stays
classical at length three but not at length four.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:word-three}
If $\operatorname{char}k=0$ or $\operatorname{char}k>8$, the characteristic
polynomial of $W(a,b,c)$ is classical.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $\C\oplus\C$ put $T_x=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&L_x\\L_{\conj x}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
By~\ref{F:comp} and its polarization, $T_xT_y+T_yT_x=B(x,y)I$, so $x\mapsto T_x$
makes $\C\oplus\C$ the $16$-dimensional spin module of the Clifford algebra of
$(\C,N)$. For vectors $x_1,\dots,x_{2m}$, the trace $\tr(T_{x_1}\cdots T_{x_{2m}})$
is $16$ times the scalar part of $x_1\cdots x_{2m}$, a polynomial in the
$B(x_i,x_j)$. The grading operator $\varepsilon=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$
anticommutes with every $T_x$, so it is a multiple of the volume element. Hence
$\tr(\varepsilon T_{x_1}\cdots T_{x_{2m}})=0$ whenever the $x_i$ lie in a subspace
of dimension less than $8$.

Let $v=(ab)c$ and $X=T_aT_{\conj b}T_cT_v$. Then $X$ is block diagonal with upper
block $L_aL_bL_cL_{\conj v}=N(v)\,W(a,b,c)$. So
$\tr W^k=N(v)^{-k}\cdot\tfrac12\tr(X^k)$, a polynomial in the inner products
of $a$, $\conj b$, $c$ and $v$ (these span at most four dimensions). Each of
these is classical. This is clear for those not involving $v$, and
$B(v,v)=2N(a)N(b)N(c)$, $B(c,v)=N(c)T(ab)$, $B(a,v)=N(a)B(\conj c,b)$, and
\[
  B(\conj b,v)=T\bigl((bab)c\bigr)=B(a,\conj b)\,T(bc)-N(b)\,T(\conj ac),
\]
by the identities before Lemma~\ref{lem:skew}, associativity of the trace,
and $bab=B(a,\conj b)b-N(b)\conj a$. Thus every power sum of the eigenvalues of
$W(a,b,c)$ is classical. Newton's identities recover the characteristic
polynomial when $8!$ is invertible in $k$.
\end{proof}

The same argument applies to $v=a(bc)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:word-four}
Over $\F_3$, let $a_1=e_1$, $a_2=e_2$, $a_3=e_3$, $a_4=1+e_4$ in the model
\eqref{eq:cd}, and $h=s_{e_1}s_{e_1+e_4}$. Then $h\in\SO(\C,N)$ fixes $1$, so
$(1,ha_1,\dots,ha_4)$ has the same Gram matrix as $(1,a_1,\dots,a_4)$, but
$W(a_1,\dots,a_4)$ and $W(ha_1,\dots,ha_4)$ have different characteristic
polynomials. In particular, word defects of length $4$ are not classical.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A direct computation in exact arithmetic
(\texttt{experiments/n2/word\_defects.py}) gives the characteristic
polynomials $X^8+2X^6+2X^2+1$ and $X^8+X^4+1$.
\end{proof}

The same script draws $120$ random words of each length $n\in\{2,3,4,5\}$ over
$\F_3$, $\F_5$ and $\F_7$, together with a random rotation of $1^\perp$. The
characteristic polynomial never changes for $n\le3$, and it changes for
$88$ to $117$ of the $120$ words for each $n\in\{4,5\}$ (data file
\texttt{data/n2/}\allowbreak\texttt{word-defects-v1.json}). A plausible mechanism is that, from
length four on, inner products of the letters with the value $v$ involve the
associator through the four-form $B(\assoc xyz,w)$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:skew}). That form is invariant under automorphisms of $\C$ but
not under $\SO(1^\perp)$.

\begin{remark}
Corollary~\ref{cor:class}(a) gives a second proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-nondeg}: $\rho-I$ is right multiplication by $g-1$,
which is invertible because $N(g-1)=N(g)-T(g)+1=2-\tau=N(c)/\nu\neq0$.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{What the defect class sees}
Remark~\ref{rem:unipotent} and Proposition~\ref{prop:word-four} locate the
non-classical defect data. This subsection identifies it: for a single defect
it is exactly $\dim Q$, and for words it appears to be the
$\operatorname{Aut}(\C)$-invariant three- and four-forms of the letters.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:jordan}
Let $\nu\neq0$ and $A=\Delta(x,y)-I$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $(\Delta-I)(\Delta^2-\tau\Delta+I)=0$.
\item If $\tau=-2$, then $\Delta$ is semisimple if and only if $xy+yx=0$, which
  holds if and only if $T(x)=T(y)=B(x,y)=0$. Otherwise $\Delta+I$ has rank $2$
  and square $0$, so $\Delta$ has two Jordan blocks of size $2$ at $-1$.
\item If $N(c)=0$, then $A^3=0$, and: $A=0$ if $\dim Q\le2$; $\rank A=2$ and
  $A^2=0$ if $\dim Q=3$; and $\rank A=4$, $\rank A^2=\rank B|_Q\in\{1,2\}$ if
  $\dim Q=4$.
\end{enumerate}
So the unipotent Jordan types are $1^8$, $2^21^4$, $3^21^2$ (when
$\rank B|_Q=2$) and $3\,2^2\,1$ (when $\rank B|_Q=1$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(a) Let $M=\nu\Delta=L_xL_yL_{\conj{xy}}$. Then
$\nu^3(\Delta-I)(\Delta^2-\tau\Delta+I)=(M-\nu I)(M^2-(2\nu-N(c))M+\nu^2I)$
is a polynomial in $(x,y)$. As in Lemma~\ref{lem:gram}, it suffices to show
that it vanishes where $\nu N(c)\neq0$. There, by the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:charpoly}, $M=\nu$ on $Q$, and on $Qv$ it is right
multiplication by $h$ with $h^2-T(h)h+N(h)=0$, $T(h)=2\nu-N(c)$ and
$N(h)=\nu^2$.

(b) If $\tau=-2$, then $N(c)=4\nu\neq0$, so Corollary~\ref{cor:class}(a)
applies: $\Delta=\mathrm{id}_Q\oplus\rho$, and $\rho$ is right multiplication
by $g$ with $(g+1)^2=g^2+2g+1=(\tau+2)g=0$. If $g=-1$, then $\rho=-I$.
Otherwise $m=g+1$ is nonzero with $m^2=0$. After extending scalars,
$Q\cong M_2$, $m$ is a matrix of rank $1$, and right multiplication by $m$ has
rank $2$ and square $0$. Since $g=(xy)^{-1}yx$, $g=-1$ means $yx=-xy$. Write
$x=\alpha1+x_0$ and $y=\beta1+y_0$ with $x_0,y_0$ pure. Then $c=x_0y_0-y_0x_0\neq0$,
so $x_0$ and $y_0$ are linearly independent, and
$xy+yx=(2\alpha\beta-B(x_0,y_0))1+2\alpha y_0+2\beta x_0$. This vanishes
exactly when $\alpha=\beta=0$ and $B(x_0,y_0)=0$, that is, when
$T(x)=T(y)=B(x,y)=0$.

(c) Here $\tau=2$, so (a) gives $A^3=0$. Since
$L_xL_y-L_{xy}=\varphi_{x,y}$, we have $A=\varphi L_{xy}^{-1}$ with
$\varphi=\varphi_{x,y}$. So $\ker A=L_{xy}(\ker\varphi)$ and
$\operatorname{im}A=\operatorname{im}\varphi=(\ker\varphi)^\perp$, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:skew}. If $\dim Q\le2$, then $\varphi=0$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:rank}). If $\dim Q=3$, then $\rank A=2$, and in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:rank-three}, with $(n,t)$ the basis of $Q_0$ chosen
there, we have $n\C\subseteq\ker\varphi$. As $n\C$ is
totally isotropic of dimension $4$, $(n\C)^\perp=n\C$, so
$\operatorname{im}A\subseteq n\C$. Moreover $Q\cdot n\C\subseteq n\C$: indeed
$n(nz)=n^2z=0$, and linearizing the left alternative law gives
$t(nz)=-n(tz)+(tn+nt)z=-n(tz)$, since $tn+nt=-B(t,n)1=0$ for
$n\in Q\cap Q^\perp$. As $L_{xy}^{-1}=\nu^{-1}L_{\conj{xy}}$ with
$\conj{xy}\in Q$, we get $L_{xy}^{-1}(\operatorname{im}A)\subseteq
n\C\subseteq\ker\varphi$, so $A^2=0$. If $\dim Q=4$, then $\ker\varphi=Q$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-four}), so $\operatorname{im}A=Q^\perp$. Also
$\ker A=L_{xy}Q=Q$, because $Q$ is an associative subalgebra containing $xy$
and $N(xy)\neq0$. Hence
$\rank A^2=\dim A(Q^\perp)=4-\dim(Q^\perp\cap Q)=\rank B|_Q$. By the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:assoc}, $Q/(Q\cap Q^\perp)$ is a composition algebra, of
dimension $1$, $2$ or $4$, and the last is excluded because $B|_Q$ is
degenerate (Lemma~\ref{lem:gram}). The Jordan types follow from the ranks of
the powers of $A$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:gram-dimq}
Let $\nu\neq0$. The similarity class of $\Delta(x,y)$ depends only on the Gram
matrix of $(1,x,y)$ and on $\dim Q$. The dimension of $Q$ matters only when
$N(c)=0$, and there it is not determined by the Gram matrix
(Remark~\ref{rem:unipotent}).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:classical}, $\tau$ is Gram data. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:jordan}(b), so is semisimplicity when $\tau=-2$. When
$N(c)=0$, the Gram matrix of $(1,x,y,xy)$ is Gram data too, since
$B(1,xy)=B(y,\conj x)=T(x)T(y)-B(x,y)$, $B(x,xy)=N(x)T(y)$,
$B(y,xy)=N(y)T(x)$ and $B(xy,xy)=2\nu$. So is $\rank B|_Q$ when $\dim Q=4$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:jordan} lists the classes.
\end{proof}

So a single defect carries exactly one datum beyond its Gram matrix, namely
$\dim Q$. For words, the group to compare with is
$G_2=\operatorname{Aut}(\C)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:equivariant}
For $g\in\operatorname{Aut}(\C)$ and letters of nonzero norm,
\[
  W(ga_1,\dots,ga_n)=g\,W(a_1,\dots,a_n)\,g^{-1}.
\]
Hence the characteristic polynomial of a word defect is invariant under
$\operatorname{Aut}(\C)$. This group fixes $1$ and preserves $B$ and, on
$1^\perp$, the three-form $\phi(p,q,r)=B(pq,r)$ and the four-form
$\psi(p,q,r,s)=B(\assoc pqr,s)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$gL_ag^{-1}=L_{ga}$, and $g$ maps the left-comb value of the word to that of
the moved word. Automorphisms preserve $N$, because $N(a)$ is the constant
term of the minimal polynomial of $a$, and therefore they preserve $B$, $1^\perp$, $\phi$ and
$\psi$.
\end{proof}

A rotation of $1^\perp$ preserves $B$ but in general neither form. This
suggests the following conjecture.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:g2}
The characteristic polynomial of $W(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ depends only on the Gram
matrix of $(1,a_1,\dots,a_n)$ and on the values of $\phi$ on triples and $\psi$
on quadruples of the trace-zero parts of the letters.
\end{conjecture}

The script \texttt{experiments/n2/word\_g2.py} applies random rotations $h$ of
$1^\perp$ to random words of length $4$ over $\F_3$, $\F_5$ and $\F_7$, and of
length $5$ over $\F_3$. All $979$ rotations that preserve every value of both
$\phi$ and $\psi$ also preserve the characteristic polynomial. Neither form
suffices alone: $344$ of $396$ rotations that preserve $\phi$ but not $\psi$,
and $202$ of $240$ sampled rotations that preserve $\psi$ but not $\phi$,
change it. The data file stores one certificate for each case. Over an
algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$, the conjecture would
follow from a first fundamental theorem for $G_2$ whose generators are $B$,
$\phi$ and $\psi$, as in Schwarz~\cite{schwarz1988}. We have not checked that
statement and do not use it. A word defect therefore sees octonionic data,
not only orthogonal data, and $\psi$, the form that measures the associator,
is needed.

\section{The associator pentagon}\label{sec:pentagon}

Let $a,b,c,d$ be elements of an arbitrary (not necessarily associative)
algebra over a commutative ring. The five binary parenthesizations of $abcd$,
\[
  v_0=((ab)c)d,\quad v_1=(a(bc))d,\quad v_2=a((bc)d),\quad
  v_3=a(b(cd)),\quad v_4=(ab)(cd),
\]
form the vertices of a pentagon, the two-dimensional associahedron
\cite{stasheff1963,maclane1963}. The edge differences $e_i=v_{i+1}-v_i$
(indices modulo~$5$) are associators:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:edges}
  e_0=\assoc abc\,d,\quad e_1=\assoc{a}{bc}{d},\quad e_2=a\,\assoc bcd,\quad
  e_3=-\assoc{a}{b}{cd},\quad e_4=-\assoc{ab}{c}{d}.
\end{equation}
Since $\sum_ie_i$ telescopes,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:teich}
  \assoc abc\,d+\assoc{a}{bc}{d}+a\,\assoc bcd-\assoc{a}{b}{cd}-\assoc{ab}{c}{d}=0,
\end{equation}
which is the Teichm\"uller identity~\cite{zhevlakov1982}. In a
nonassociative algebra the individual edges may be nonzero, so the identity
records non-associativity as edge data on a closed boundary.

\begin{observation}\label{obs:weak}
As a test of an implementation of~\eqref{eq:edges}, the closure
\eqref{eq:teich} is weak in two specific ways.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item It holds in every bilinear algebra. An implementation that computes all
  products in the wrong algebra therefore still closes, for example the
  opposite algebra obtained by transposing the structure tensor.
\item Replacing $\assoc xyz$ by $-\assoc xyz$ negates every edge and preserves
  the closure.
\end{enumerate}
Detecting (a) requires comparison with an independent computation in the
intended algebra. Detecting (b) requires checking each edge against its
vertex difference $v_{i+1}-v_i$, which is computed without associators.
\end{observation}

\section{Computational certification}\label{sec:census}

\subsection{Defect census}
For each $\ell\in\{3,5,7,11\}$ we draw $400$ pairs $(x,y)$ of nonzero-norm
elements of $\C_\ell$ uniformly with a fixed seed. We add degenerate pairs
$y=\alpha1+\beta x$ ($\beta\neq0$, $N(y)\neq0$) for a quarter of the $x$.
Each pair is checked exactly in $\F_\ell$ for:
\begin{itemize}
\item the inverse formula;
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(a)--(c);
\item $\det\Delta=1$;
\item $\Delta|_Q=\mathrm{id}$;
\item the rank law.
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:census} reports the strata. No check failed. On the
$\dim Q=4$ stratum, $\tr\Delta$ takes every value in $\F_\ell$, so the trace is
not determined by $\dim Q$. Whether the class of $\Delta|_{Q^\perp}$ carries
arithmetic information beyond classical conjugacy data is the open question of
Section~\ref{sec:open}. Under uniform sampling the stratum $\dim Q=3$ is rare
(six pairs in total) and absent for $\ell\ge7$, so the census says little about
the middle case of Theorem~\ref{thm:rank}. Over $\F_3$ exhaustion fills that
gap.

\begin{proposition}[exhaustive verification]\label{prop:f3}
The rank law of Theorem~\ref{thm:rank} holds for every pair $x,y$ in the split octonion
algebra over $\F_3$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:phi}(b), $\rank(\Delta-I)=\rank\varphi_{x,y}$. Both $\varphi_{x,y}$ (up to a nonzero
scalar) and $Q$ are unchanged by $x\mapsto\lambda x+\alpha$,
$y\mapsto\mu y+\beta$ with $\lambda\mu\neq0$, because the associator is
trilinear and vanishes on scalars. They are also unchanged by swapping $x$ and
$y$. If $1,x,y$ are linearly dependent, Proposition~\ref{prop:flat} applies.
Otherwise the pair reduces to two distinct points of the projective space of
trace-zero elements, which has $(3^7-1)/2=1093$ points. A computation in exact
arithmetic over all $\binom{1093}{2}=596{,}778$ unordered pairs finds
$\rank\varphi=2$ for each of the $21{,}840$ pairs with $\dim Q=3$ and
$\rank\varphi=4$ for each of the $574{,}938$ pairs with $\dim Q=4$
(\texttt{experiments/n2/rank\_law.py}).
\end{proof}

Of the pairs with $\dim Q=4$, $398{,}034$ have $B|_Q$ nondegenerate. They fall
under Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-nondeg}. The remaining $176{,}904$, and all
$21{,}840$ pairs with $\dim Q=3$, have $B|_Q$ degenerate. They fall under
Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-four} and Proposition~\ref{prop:rank-three}, whose
proofs are independent of this computation.

\subsection{A Lean oracle for the pentagon}
The oracle, written in Lean~4~\cite{moura2021}, takes a modulus
$p>1$, an explicit structure tensor $t_{ijk}$ (the coefficient of $e_k$ in
$e_ie_j$) and four vectors. It recomputes every product from the tensor
alone. Its self-check runs on two models:
\begin{itemize}
\item an $\F_3$ octonion tensor with a basis witness;
\item an $\F_5$ Cayley--Dickson tensor generated, together with three input
  quadruples and their edge values, by an independent Python implementation
  of~\eqref{eq:cd}.
\end{itemize}
Two of the $\F_5$ quadruples have all five edges nonzero.

For each $\F_5$ case the self-check requires the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{closure}: \eqref{eq:teich} holds;
\item \emph{replay}: the five edges equal the independently computed values;
\item \emph{vertex}: each edge equals its vertex difference;
\item \emph{sign flip}: negating edge $i$ breaks the closure exactly when
  $e_i\neq0$ (true for odd $p$, since the closure then changes by $2e_i$);
\item in cases with all edges nonzero, moving the outer factor of
  $\assoc abc d$ or $a\assoc bcd$ to the other side breaks the closure.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:mutants} lists six single-fragment mutants of the oracle source
and the check families that detect each one. Every mutant is detected. The
two classes of Observation~\ref{obs:weak} behave as predicted:
\begin{itemize}
\item the globally negated associator preserves closure, and is detected only
  by replay, the vertex check, and the pinned $\F_3$ witness;
\item the transposed tensor (the opposite algebra) passes closure and the
  vertex check, and is detected \emph{only} by replay against the independent
  implementation.
\end{itemize}
The basis quadruples $(e_0,e_1,e_2,e_4)$ of both models, whose first factor is
the identity, detect none of the four single-edge mutants. Inputs with every edge
nonzero are necessary.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
All data files are generated deterministically and checked byte for byte in
continuous integration. One portability defect is worth recording. Since
version~3.12, Python's built-in \texttt{sum} uses compensated summation over
floats, so descriptive statistics computed under 3.11 and 3.12 differ in the
last bits. A statistic that is mathematically zero can come out as $0$ under
one version and as $\approx10^{-31}$ under the other. Committed
floating-point summaries are therefore rounded to $10$ decimal places and
$12$ significant digits before hashing. The exact integer data (orbit counts,
strata, edges) are unaffected.

Claims are recorded in a content-addressed ledger of proof records, which
distinguishes verified finite computations, repository theorems, imported
theorems, bounded experiments and pending statements. A claim-governance
check keeps the ledger consistent with its rendered surfaces and requires a
test for every claim marked proved. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} lists the
artifacts.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Composition algebras and their classification go back to
Hurwitz~\cite{hurwitz1898}. Standard references are Schafer~\cite{schafer1966}
and Springer--Veldkamp~\cite{springer2000}, and the surveys of
Baez~\cite{baez2002} and Conway--Smith~\cite{conway2003} cover normalized left
multiplications, triality and $\mathrm{Spin}(8)$. Artin's theorem and the
Teichm\"uller identity are classical~\cite{schafer1966,zhevlakov1982}. The
pentagon of parenthesizations is the associahedron of
Stasheff~\cite{stasheff1963} and the coherence pentagon of
Mac~Lane~\cite{maclane1963}. Here it appears in its simplest, linear form.
Mutation analysis as a measure of test adequacy goes back to DeMillo, Lipton
and Sayward~\cite{demillo1978}.

We expect that statements equivalent to Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:det} are known to specialists in some form, possibly
phrased via triality or the Moufang identities. A systematic, dated search of
the literature on triality, octonionic loops, and defect or holonomy
constructions has not yet been carried out. Until it is, the paper claims
only the exact finite formulation, the explicit proofs, and the certification
methodology.

\section{Limitations and open problems}\label{sec:open}

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \emph{What the defect class can still carry.} The similarity class
  of a single defect is the Gram matrix of $(1,x,y)$ together with $\dim Q$
  (Corollary~\ref{cor:gram-dimq}). Over $\F_q$ the $\SO(\C,N)$-class of a
  semisimple defect depends only on $\tau$, and the spinor norm is trivial
  (Corollary~\ref{cor:so-class}). The $\SO(\C,N)$-classes of non-semisimple
  defects are not determined here. Words of length three have classical
  characteristic polynomials (Proposition~\ref{prop:word-three}), but words
  of length four do not (Proposition~\ref{prop:word-four}).
  Conjecture~\ref{conj:g2} asserts that $G_2$ data suffices. A proof over
  finite fields, or a reference for the first fundamental theorem it would
  follow from in characteristic~$0$, is open.
\item \emph{Defect-stratified statistics.} For navigation words in generators
  drawn from a prime-norm shell modulo $\ell$, do collision moments
  conditioned on the classes of their word defects differ from the
  unconditioned moments? For single defects the only non-classical stratum
  is $\dim Q$ when $N(xy-yx)=0$. For words of length at least four, the
  conditioning variables to test are the values of $\phi$ and $\psi$ on the
  letters. A negative answer would show that the defect is classical
  bookkeeping, or $G_2$ bookkeeping, only.
\item \emph{Formal soundness.} The oracle decides finite instances by
  evaluation. A kernel-checked theorem connecting its Boolean checks to the
  composition-algebra structures of Lean's mathematical library is not yet
  available.
\item \emph{Scope of the census.} Sample sizes are small and seeded. The
  census establishes no asymptotic or distributional statement.
\end{enumerate}
